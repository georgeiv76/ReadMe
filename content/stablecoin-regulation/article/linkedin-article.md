# The Underlying Currency Dominance War in Stablecoin Regulation: MiCA and the GENIUS Act

*Two landmark laws, one unspoken battlefield. Strip away the compliance details and stablecoin regulation is monetary geopolitics by other means.*

---

On July 18, 2025, the United States signed the GENIUS Act into law. A year earlier, on June 30, 2024, the stablecoin titles of Europe's MiCA regulation went live. Read side by side, they look like siblings: both demand 1:1 reserves, both ban paying interest to holders, both force redemption at par, both put issuers under bank-style supervision.

Read them again, and they are opposing war plans.

## The number that explains everything

Roughly **99% of the ~$300 billion stablecoin market is pegged to the US dollar**. Euro stablecoins? About **€450 million — less than 0.2% of the market**. That's not a market share. That's a rout.

And the money is not idle. Under its December 2025 attestation, Tether alone held about **$122 billion in US Treasury bills — making it the 17th-largest holder of US government debt, ahead of Germany**. Add Circle's ~$48 billion and stablecoin issuers already rival mid-sized sovereigns as financiers of the US government.

Once you see that, both laws snap into focus.

## The GENIUS Act: offense

Washington did not regulate stablecoins to contain them. It regulated them to **weaponize them**.

The Act's reserve rules allow essentially one thing: cash, insured deposits, and **US Treasuries with maturities of 93 days or less** (plus repos and government money market funds built on the same). Every dollar of global stablecoin growth is now structurally channeled into short-dated US debt. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the quiet part out loud at signing: stablecoins "will buttress the dollar's status as the global reserve currency… and lead to a surge in demand for US Treasuries." He projects a **$3.7 trillion market by 2030**. Standard Chartered translates a $2 trillion market into **~$1.6 trillion of new T-bill demand — roughly all the net new bills the US plans to issue** in that window. Citi thinks issuers could out-hold every foreign sovereign creditor by 2030.

This is the eurodollar playbook, updated. In the 1960s-70s, offshore dollar markets entrenched the dollar's dominance far beyond US borders. Stablecoins are Eurodollars 2.0 — except this time the reserves sit in T-bills by statute, the rail is a smartphone, and adoption is strongest exactly where the dollar wins by default: in a Castle Island survey across five emerging markets, **47% of stablecoin users said their primary reason is simply "to access dollars."** Argentina, Turkey, Nigeria — digital dollarization is already happening; the GENIUS Act just gave it a federal charter.

## MiCA: defense

Now look at what Europe built. MiCA's stablecoin regime reads like a fortification manual:

- **Article 23 usage caps** (extended to non-euro tokens via Article 58(3)): if a foreign-currency stablecoin used as a means of exchange exceeds **1 million transactions and €200 million per day** in the euro area, the issuer must *stop issuing* and file a plan to shrink. There is no equivalent anywhere in the GENIUS Act — because the US has nothing to defend against.
- **Reserve localization**: 30% of reserves in EU bank deposits, rising to **60% for "significant" tokens** — the rule Tether's Paolo Ardoino called a systemic risk, and the stated reason Tether refused to seek authorization at all. Result: USDT, the world's largest stablecoin, was delisted for EU users by Coinbase, Kraken, Crypto.com and Binance by March 2025.
- The **ECB's veto instincts**: an April 2025 ECB non-paper fought "multi-issuance" (the same token minted inside and outside the EU) on the grounds that a global run would drain EU-held reserves. Christine Lagarde calls dollar stablecoins a "privatization of money" and warned in May 2026 of the "**digital dollarisation**" of Europe. ECB board member Piero Cipollone is more explicit: US stablecoin policy could pull euro deposits to America and "compromise our monetary sovereignty."

And the counterattack: the **digital euro**. The ECB closed its preparation phase in October 2025; the European Parliament's economic committee adopted its position in June 2026 — reported in the European press, without embarrassment, as a project "to reduce US dominance in payments." Pilot in 2027, possible issuance by 2029.

## Same tools, opposite doctrines

Here's the elegant irony: the two laws use nearly identical instruments for opposite strategic ends.

| Instrument | GENIUS (US) | MiCA (EU) |
|---|---|---|
| 1:1 reserves | Channel global demand into T-bills | Keep money and risk inside EU banks |
| Interest ban | Protect US bank deposits at home | Protect EU bank deposits at home |
| Foreign-issuer rules | Reciprocity to *export* the dollar rail | Caps and localization to *block* the dollar rail |
| End goal | Dollar everywhere | Euro survives at home |

The US wrote an export license. Europe wrote an import restriction. Both are talking about the same product: the dollar.

## What actually decides this war

**Network effects.** Payments are winner-take-most. The first credible, regulated, liquid digital-currency rail tends to keep the market — which is why a one-year head start on rulebooks matters more than any single provision.

**The multi-issuance fight.** Whether globally fungible tokens can straddle the EU border is MiCA's credibility test (the ESRB says close the door; the Commission wants it open, with a full MiCA review due by mid-2027). The answer determines whether Europe's fortress has a gate — or a breach.

**The digital euro's clock.** Euro stablecoins are 0.2% of the market and the public alternative won't circulate before 2029 at best. As one Forbes headline put it: euro stablecoins are scaling "while the digital euro waits on Brussels."

**And the skeptics deserve a hearing.** The Kansas City Fed notes stablecoins raise Treasury demand mostly by cannibalizing money market funds and deposits — substitution, not new money. The BIS argues stablecoins fail the basic tests of money (singleness, elasticity, integrity) and trade more like ETFs. USDC's depeg to $0.87 in March 2023 is a reminder that the weapon can misfire. A $100B+ T-bill fire-sale in a stablecoin run would be a US problem, not just a crypto one.

## Two questions that reveal the whole game

**Q: USDT — Paolo Ardoino's stablecoin, the largest in the world — is regulated under MiCA, right?**

No — and the way each side handled that "no" is the clearest picture of who understands the war. USDT is not just another token: it is crypto's **unit of account** — the most common trading pair on earth, the currency in which the market quotes itself. MiCA offered its issuer a **binary choice**: comply now (including parking up to 60% of reserves in EU bank deposits, a rule Ardoino called a bank-run risk) or leave. Tether left. USDT was delisted across Coinbase, Kraken, Crypto.com and Binance for EU users by March 2025 — and global liquidity didn't move to Europe's compliant alternatives; it simply carried on trading in USDT *outside* Europe. By building a wall instead of a bridge, MiCA didn't regulate crypto's main trading currency — it cut the European Union out of it.

Washington read the same problem and built the opposite structure: a **bridge, with a clock on it**. USDT is fully legal in the US today. The Act's issuance rules bite in January 2027, but the real deadline is **July 18, 2028** — a three-year transition after which US exchanges must delist any stablecoin without a permitted issuer. Three years to adapt, not ninety days to comply. And crucially, the Act **left the door open for foreign regulation**: under **Section 18**, a foreign-regulated stablecoin keeps its US access if Treasury deems its home regime "comparable" and the issuer registers with the OCC and holds US-based reserves — turning comparability into a diplomatic instrument, an invitation for other jurisdictions to align with US standards rather than be shut out. Tether is using every lane of that bridge: lobbying for the Section 18 route for USDT (now headquartered in El Salvador) while launching **USAT** (January 2026, issued by the federally chartered Anchorage Digital Bank) as its GENIUS-native token. Same "non-compliant" coin, two regulatory philosophies: Europe got a cleaner rulebook and lost the asset; America got three years of leverage over the asset — and over every foreign regulator who wants access to its market.

**Q: Could you issue a *euro* stablecoin under the GENIUS Act?**

On paper, yes. The Act nowhere says "dollar" — a payment stablecoin is defined by its peg to "monetary value," a term broad enough to cover any national currency, and there is no clause forbidding a US-licensed issuer from pegging to the euro. But now read the reserve rules, because that's where the trap is. The permitted-reserve list is **exhaustive**, and every item on it is American: US coins and currency, deposits at insured US banks, **US Treasuries with maturities of 93 days or less**, repos backed by those same Treasuries, government money market funds holding the same, and central bank reserve deposits. The only interest-bearing assets a GENIUS issuer may hold are **US government debt**. No German Bubills, no French BTFs, no euro sovereign paper of any kind — and there is no "euro T-bill" to ask for anyway, because the ECB does not issue debt; the euro area has no unified short-term safe asset, only fragmented national bills.

Why does this kill the euro coin? Because of the other rule both laws share: **issuers cannot pay yield to holders**. Which means the interest earned on reserves is not a nice-to-have — it is the *entire business model*. Tether's ~$10B+ profit in 2025 and Circle's revenue are, at bottom, T-bill coupons on other people's money. A euro stablecoin under the GENIUS Act would face an impossible choice: hold dollar T-bills against a euro peg (a currency mismatch that breaks the coin), or hold non-earning euro cash (a business with no revenue). The dollar coin gets a yield engine by statute; the euro coin gets a cost center.

And note what this is *not*: it is not a restriction on who or where. MiCA works the same way in mirror image — it doesn't care where the parent company sits (American, Japanese, anyone can issue, through an EU-licensed entity); what it regulates is **the reserve**. MiCA does let issuers earn on euro-area national bills via the EBA's highly-liquid-assets list — but it forces 30-60% of reserves into low-earning bank deposits, and euro short rates run at roughly half of dollar rates to begin with. So the reserve rules, on both sides of the Atlantic, are the actual battlefield: US law converts every stablecoin it touches into a buyer of US debt and makes a euro coin under US law economically pointless, while EU law makes the euro coin legally possible but commercially handicapped. Nobody banned anybody's currency. They just wrote the reserve rules so their own debt wins.

## The uncomfortable conclusion

Currencies don't compete on ideology; they compete on **distribution**. The GENIUS Act turned every wallet on earth into a potential distribution point for the dollar and every stablecoin issuer into a captive buyer of US debt. MiCA — prudent, sophisticated, and defensive — regulates the products of a currency area whose stablecoins barely exist.

The war isn't between regulators and crypto. It's between currencies, fought through regulation. And so far, only one side is playing offense.
